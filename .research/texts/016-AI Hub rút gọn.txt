I would like to start with the AI Hub project, one of the most important projects I worked on at Samsung R&D Vietnam.
AI Hub was developed based on requirements from Samsung Headquarters in Korea. I was fortunate to be trusted as the Lead Business Analyst for this project.
In simple terms, AI Hub was an internal AI asset lifecycle management platform. It was also part of the broader Samsung AI Platform ecosystem. The main purpose of AI Hub was to help AI teams manage datasets, AI models, training workflows, evaluation results, and related documents in a secure and standard way.
Before AI Hub, datasets and models could be stored separately by different teams. It was difficult to know who owned a dataset, which version was the latest, which dataset was used to train which model, and whether the data was approved for AI training. This could create problems in traceability, reuse, quality control, and compliance.
This was very important because AI training data can create legal and security risks. If a team uses unauthorized, sensitive, copyrighted, or legally restricted data for AI training, it may lead to privacy issues, intellectual property disputes, regulatory problems, or even lawsuits. So AI Hub was not only a storage system. It was a governance platform for AI assets.
To solve these problems, AI Hub had several core modules.
The first module was Dataset Management. Each dataset had a standard Dataset Card. It stored key information such as owner, sponsor, department, data type, schema, sensitivity level, usage scope, version, labels, and related models. The system also supported dataset templates, so different data types, such as tabular data, image data, text data, or log data, could follow the same registration standard.
The second module was Data Source Management. This module helped control where the data came from. The source could be an internal database, API, file storage, data warehouse, external vendor, or purchased data package. Some sensitive actions, such as uploading new files, changing source connections, downloading raw data, or deleting source data, had to go through approval.
The third module was Approval Workflow Management. It was used to control important actions, such as asset purchase requests, dataset or model registration, sharing requests, edit or delete requests, data source approval, training job approval, and model deployment approval. The workflow supported multi-level approval, parallel approval, conditional routing, comments, rejection, change requests, SLA tracking, and notifications.
Another important module was Dataset and Model Lifecycle Status. The system managed statuses such as Draft, Under Review, Approved, Published, In Use, Deprecated, Archived, Staging, and Production. These statuses were important because they controlled what users could do at each stage.
AI Hub also had Permission and Access Control. It controlled who could view, edit, use, share, download, approve, or deploy AI assets. The permission logic was based on role, department, owner, sensitivity level, asset status, and usage scope.
Other modules supported the full AI lifecycle. Model Registry helped manage model information, model versions, and related datasets. Dataset Catalog and Search helped users find reusable datasets. Training Job Management helped users request and track training jobs. Evaluation and Benchmarking helped compare model performance. Audit Trail helped track important user actions. Dashboard Reporting helped managers monitor datasets, models, approvals, and governance status.
As the Lead Business Analyst, I was involved throughout the full software development process for about eight months, from the early idea stage to handover and pilot deployment. My main responsibilities included:
Working with Korean experts, PM, developers, testers, and internal stakeholders to collect and clarify requirements.
Analyzing business problems and proposing practical solutions.
Defining the feature list, priority, scope, and product roadmap.
Writing BRD, SRS, FRD, user stories, acceptance criteria, business rules, and exception flows.
Drawing process flows, data flows, activity diagrams, ERD, and lifecycle status flows.
Designing wireframes, mockups, and screen flows for dataset, model, data source, approval, permission, catalog, dashboard, and asset detail pages.
Mapping APIs from Swagger documents to each screen and feature.
Designing Dataset Templates and Model Templates, including required fields, optional fields, data types, validation rules, and field dependencies.
Designing the permission matrix based on role, department, owner, sensitivity level, usage scope, and asset status.
Designing reusable approval workflows for dataset registration, model registration, sharing, edit or delete requests, data source approval, training job approval, and model deployment approval.
Designing dataset and model lifecycle statuses and defining which actions were allowed at each status.
Analyzing Training Job, Evaluation, and Model Deployment flows.
Designing audit trail, notification, and dashboard requirements.
Defining data governance rules for metadata, ownership, sensitivity level, usage scope, lineage, retention, sharing, download, and audit control.
Defining traceability rules between Dataset, Model, Training Job, Evaluation, and Deployment.
Defining risk control rules for sensitive actions, such as downloading raw data, changing data sources, deleting assets, sharing restricted datasets, or deploying models to production.
In this project, I worked closely with Korean experts who had strong experience in Data Governance, Master Data Management, and AI platform operations. Every three to four months, they came to Vietnam for about one week to present key requirements and discuss the future direction of the platform. During those sessions, we clarified business needs, discussed solution options, finalized the scope, and aligned on the development roadmap.
After each onsite session, I continued to work with them remotely. Usually, the PM and I had one or two meetings with the Korean team every week to update progress, clarify open questions, confirm business rules, and handle requirement changes. Most requirement confirmations were done through chat and Confluence documents, so every decision and scope change could be tracked clearly.
For me, AI Hub was a very valuable project. It was not just a normal internal system. It was a platform-level product involving AI lifecycle management, data governance, model governance, permission control, approval workflow, and cross-department collaboration. It helped me understand how a large technology company manages AI assets in a secure, scalable, traceable, and compliant way.
