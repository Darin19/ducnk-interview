I would like to start with the AI Hub project, one of the most important projects I worked on at Samsung R&D Vietnam.
AI Hub was developed based on requirements from Samsung Headquarters in Korea. I was fortunate to be trusted as the Lead Business Analyst for this project. In simple terms, AI Hub was an internal AI asset lifecycle management platform and also part of the broader Samsung AI Platform ecosystem.
The main purpose of AI Hub was to help AI teams manage datasets, AI models, training workflows, evaluation results, and related documents in a secure, standardized, and reusable way. Without this platform, datasets and models could be scattered across different teams, making it difficult to track ownership, version history, data usage, and compliance. This was especially important because using unauthorized, sensitive, copyrighted, or legally restricted datasets for AI training could create serious risks, including privacy violations, intellectual property disputes, regulatory issues, and even lawsuits.
To solve these problems, AI Hub was built with several core modules: Dataset Management, Data Source Management, Approval Workflow Management, Dataset and Model Lifecycle Status, and Permission and Access Control. Besides these core modules, the system also included Model Registry, Dataset Catalog and Search, Training Job Management, Evaluation and Benchmarking, Notification Templates, Audit Trail, and Dashboard Reporting.
With Dataset Management, each dataset had a standard Dataset Card. It stored key information such as owner, sponsor, department, data type, schema, sensitivity level, usage scope, version, labels, and related models. The system also supported dataset templates, so different types of data, such as tabular data, image data, text data, or log data, could follow the same registration standard.
Data Source Management helped control where the data came from. The source could be an internal database, API, file storage, data warehouse, external vendor, or purchased data package. Some sensitive actions, such as uploading new files, changing source connections, downloading raw data, or deleting source data, had to go through approval.
Approval Workflow Management was used to control important actions, such as asset purchase requests, dataset or model registration, sharing requests, edit or delete requests, data source approval, training job approval, and model deployment approval. The workflow supported multi-level approval, parallel approval, conditional routing, automatic approver assignment, comments, rejection, change requests, SLA tracking, and notifications.
Dataset and Model Lifecycle Status helped the system control the status of each asset, such as Draft, Under Review, Approved, Published, In Use, Deprecated, Archived, Staging, and Production. These statuses were important because they decided what users could do at each stage.
Permission and Access Control helped control who could view, edit, use, share, download, approve, or deploy AI assets. The permission logic was based on role, department, owner, sensitivity level, asset status, and usage scope.
Other modules also supported the full AI lifecycle. Model Registry helped manage model information, model versions, and related datasets. Dataset Catalog and Search helped users find reusable datasets. Training Job Management helped users request and track training jobs. Evaluation and Benchmarking helped compare model performance. Audit Trail helped track who created, edited, deleted, shared, downloaded, or approved an asset. Dashboard Reporting helped managers monitor datasets, models, approvals, and governance status.
Besides the system features, as the Lead Business Analyst, my main responsibilities included:
Working with Korean experts, PM, developers, testers, and internal stakeholders to collect and clarify requirements.
Analyzing business problems and proposing practical solutions, instead of only writing down user requests.
Defining the feature list, priority, scope, and product roadmap.
Writing BRD, SRS, FRD, user stories, acceptance criteria, business rules, and exception flows.
Drawing business and system diagrams, such as process flows, data flows, activity diagrams, ERD, and lifecycle status flows.
Designing wireframes, mockups, and screen flows for dataset, model, data source, approval, permission, catalog, dashboard, and asset detail pages.
Mapping APIs from Swagger documents to each screen and feature, including request, response, fields, rules, error cases, and missing gaps.
Designing Dataset Templates and Model Templates, including required fields, optional fields, data types, validation rules, and field dependencies.
Designing the permission matrix based on role, department, owner, sponsor, contributor, sensitivity level, usage scope, and asset status.
Designing reusable approval workflows for purchase requests, dataset/model registration, sharing, edit/delete, data source approval, training job approval, and model deployment approval.
Designing dataset and model lifecycle statuses and defining which actions were allowed at each status.
Analyzing Data Source Management, including source type, uploaded files, API/database connections, and actions such as view, edit, upload, update, download, and delete.
Designing Dataset Catalog and Search logic, including search, filter, sorting, tags, bookmarks, recently viewed assets, access requests, and visibility rules.
Analyzing Training Job, Evaluation, and Model Deployment flows, including dataset version selection, training parameters, job monitoring, model artifacts, evaluation metrics, and promotion to staging or production.
Designing audit trail, notification, and dashboard requirements to support tracking, reporting, and governance.
Defining data governance rules for metadata, ownership, sensitivity level, usage scope, lineage, retention, sharing, download, and audit control.
Defining traceability rules between Dataset, Model, Training Job, Evaluation, and Deployment.
Defining risk control rules for sensitive actions, such as downloading raw data, changing data sources, deleting assets, sharing restricted datasets, or deploying models to production.
In this project, I worked closely with Korean experts who had strong expertise in Data Governance, Master Data Management, and AI platform operations. Every three to four months, they came to Vietnam for about one week to present key requirements and discuss the future direction of the platform. During those sessions, we clarified business needs, discussed solution options, finalized the scope, and aligned on the development roadmap.
After each onsite working session, I continued to work with them remotely. Usually, the PM and I had one or two meetings with the Korean team every week to update progress, clarify open questions, confirm business rules, and handle requirement changes. Most of the detailed requirement confirmation was done through chat and Confluence documents, so that every decision, scope change, and business rule could be tracked clearly.
For me, AI Hub was a very valuable project because it was not just a normal internal system. It was a platform-level product involving AI lifecycle management, data governance, model governance, permission control, approval workflow, and cross-department collaboration. It helped me understand how a large technology corporation manages AI assets in a secure, scalable, traceable, and compliant way.
